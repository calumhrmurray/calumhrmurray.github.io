The SPHEREx satellite

NASA/JPL-Caltech, PIA26537: SPHEREx after environmental testing, BAE Systems, Boulder, November 2024.

The observatory a few months before launch. Right to left: the big white cone is the outer photon shield, and there are three of them nested. They are baffles, not a JWST-style sunshade: they keep sunlight and Earthshine off the telescope while it looks away from the Sun. Behind them the small drum is the telescope, 20 cm effective aperture. The red slatted panel and gold blanketing are the V-groove radiators that cool it passively; the flat black panel is the spacecraft bus. For scale (JPL press kit): 2.6 m tall, 3.2 m wide, 502 kg. A Medium-Class Explorer, not a flagship; it launched on a Falcon 9, sharing the ride with PUNCH. The 3.2 m bar is the mouth of the outer photon shield, the widest part. The 1 m bar is exact only at roughly that depth: the left end of the spacecraft is further from the camera. Slide and overlay from the CosmoStat journal-club deck.

SPHEREx scans the entire sky, at a low earth orbit

  • Launched 11 March 2025; surveying since 1 May 2025
  • 20 cm telescope, 3.5° × 11.5° field of view
  • Polar orbit, looking ~90° from the Sun: the ecliptic poles are crossed on every orbit
  • Orbits ~650 km above the Earth 

Animation: NASA/JPL-Caltech (NASA SVS), "Novel Survey Strategy". Numbers: Bock et al. 2025 (arXiv:2511.02985).

The observing geometry: the observatory works near the great circle 90° from the Sun, and that circle sweeps round the sky as the Earth goes round the Sun, so the whole sky is covered every six months. The inset is the allowed pointing zone. Sun-synchronous polar orbit, inclination 97.95°, Sun kept more than 93.5° off axis. The boresight is tipped 8° toward the solar panels, which is what lets it reach the ecliptic poles. Cooling: three conical photon shields and a three-stage V-groove radiator hold the optics near 55 K. No cryogen and no mechanical cooler, so no consumable sets the mission lifetime. The poles are crossed on nearly every orbit, so the deep fields are a gift of geometry, not an allocation. That is also why SPHEREx depth in our footprint depends on ecliptic latitude (the SPHEREx depth slide, after the release timeline). Altitude: ~650 km (NASA/JPL SPHEREx press kit, mission overview); Bock et al. 2025 Table 1 gives a semi-major axis of 7037.4 km, i.e. ~660 km above a 6378 km Earth radius, and the Explanatory Supplement the same 7037.41 km. Field of view 3.5° × 11.5° (Bock et al. 2025, §IV.3.2). Survey start 1 May 2025 (Explanatory Supplement v1.0). Hubble for comparison: ~475 km (orbital elements of June 2026, perigee 472, apogee 475 km; orbitalradar.com), launched at ~615 km in 1990 and decaying.

Euclid and SPHEREx on the same 10′ of sky

Euclid VIS + Y + H · 0.1″ pixels

SPHEREx · 6.2″ pixels

Both panels are real data of the same field: the central 10′ × 10′ of NGC 6744 (RA 287.4421, Dec −63.8574), north up, east left, on one scale, so a star in one panel is at the same place in the other. The galaxy is about 20′ across, so this is its inner disk and bulge. Euclid: the Early Release Observation (ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium), VIS + NISP Y + H through the CDS HiPS (CDS/P/Euclid/ERO), as H/Y/VIS in red/green/blue. SPHEREx: every public QR2 frame covering the nucleus, 310 in all (62, 51, 46, 57, 50, 44 visits in detectors 1-6, 113.6 s each), zodiacal-light model subtracted, sky level removed, resampled north-up onto 6.12″ pixels (native 6.15″), median-stacked per detector. Two numbers, in this order: - 6.2 arcseconds: SPHEREx pixels are 62× coarser than Euclid VIS, about 3,800 Euclid pixels in each. That is the cost. - 102 channels: every one of those pixels carries a spectrum. That is what the cost buys. All six bands share one surface-brightness scale, so the fading is real: the bulge (median within 30″ of the nucleus) is 3.2, 4.8, 4.9, 2.5, 1.7, 1.2 MJy/sr from 0.75 to 5 µm. Starlight peaks at 1-2.4 µm and is four times fainter by 5 µm. The foreground stars fade more slowly than the galaxy. The link to this talk: a typical catalogue galaxy (I_E 19-20, half-light radius ~0.75″) is a fraction of one SPHEREx pixel. SPHEREx cannot detect or deblend it; Euclid says where it is, and we measure the SPHEREx spectrum there. Why the journal-club version was replaced: it set the Euclid press image (the whole galaxy, rotated for display) beside single SPHEREx frames cut to 5′ in detector orientation, with each band stretched to its own range. Different fields and orientations, and a fade that was a change of S/N. Rebuilt by scripts/ngc6744_compare.py; its numbers are in fig/ngc6744_compare.json.

We can see airglow from Helium with SPHEREx

The low-earth orbit means that SPHEREx sees some of the atmosphere.
The sun hits He I in the upper atmosphere.
SPHEREx sees this as emission.
If we're naive it looks like H alpha

Metastable 2³S helium in the upper atmosphere resonantly scatters sunlight in the 2³S–2³P transition at 1.083 µm; Hubble's WFC3 sees it as a time-varying background (Brammer et al. 2014).

In one SPHEREx D1 frame (2025-10-20) the sky is 0.40 MJy/sr at 1.040 µm, 2.43 on the line and 0.47 at 1.106 µm. In the co-added spectra a residual on SPHEREx channels 14–15 is read as Hα at z ≈ 0.67, which made the alias pile at 0.6–0.68 and the cliff at 0.68 in the catalogue (issue #11).

The fix so far is a fit-level mask of the He I channels; the next step is SPHEREx's published He I background template (#49), and the adopted tractorjax-spherex background with 288 wavelength bins catches the line's edge across a whole frame (DECISIONS, EXT-20).

Figures: spherex-allsky results/figures/talk/he_image.png and he_spectrum.png (commit 677699d; redrawn from existing results).

Link to this catalogue, not checked: the horizontal band at z_phot ≈ 0.67 on the Euclid redshift-validation slide sits where He I read as Hα lands (1.083 / 0.6563 µm gives z ≈ 0.65), so it is probably the same line. Whether the Euclid fit masks the He I channels has not been checked.

Galaxy correlations

Eight sky maps: dust, star density, SPHEREx channel count, ecliptic latitude, zodi, background, visits, Euclid VIS depth; five are regressed.

In each shell, galaxy density is binned against each map. A trend counts only if it is 2× larger than in 400 lognormal mocks, which cluster but are clean.

Remove the worst trend with a linear fit, repeat until all pass, and weight each galaxy by 1/fit in its own shell.

What it shows

Neighbouring shells correlate (r = 0.15–0.33, the photo-z overlap); shells two or more apart are near zero.

The worst trends, removed in order

“Worst” = the map whose linear fit gains the most χ² over the mocks.

Measured on the resolved galaxy sample (release galaxy sample + not MER point_like_prob > 0.5) in six shells from 0.05 to 0.6 (universe v2_spherex_only_galaxy_resolved_z005, euclid-spherex branch v2-clustering; w(θ) job 1007282, ISD weights 1007281, mocks 1007279, randoms 1007280; numbers/numbers_v2_galaxy_resolved_clustering.json). Cross-correlations: Landy-Szalay (D_iD_j − D_iR − RD_j + RR)/RR against one shared random set (11,213,820 randoms, 10× the largest shell; uniform to χ²/pixel 0.95), each galaxy weighted by its own shell's ISD weight, 120 jackknife patches. N per shell on the mask: 232,296; 789,857; 1,087,609; 1,121,382; 715,694; 428,637. r over 10′-60′, weighted: neighbours 0.05-0.1/0.1-0.2 0.29 ± 0.04; 0.1-0.2/0.2-0.3 0.15 ± 0.04; 0.2-0.3/0.3-0.4 0.27 ± 0.03; 0.3-0.4/0.4-0.5 0.23 ± 0.03; 0.4-0.5/0.5-0.6 0.33 ± 0.03. Two or more apart: −0.03 to 0.08; 0.05-0.1 with 0.5-0.6: 0.00 ± 0.03. Unchanged within errors from the release-sample run. Reading: a neighbour's cross is the overlap of the two shells' true n(z), so r ≈ 0.15-0.33 says the photo-z leak into the next shell by tens of per cent, as expected for σ_NMAD ≈ 0.009 and Δz = 0.1 shells. Far pairs are small but not all zero: the outlier tails. In September and in the first all-sky run, bright-star halos made far shells anti-correlate; no longer. What the new first shell changed: 0.05-0.1 replaces 0-0.1 (z < 0.05 is where the point-like contamination sat). Its w(10.8′) is 0.271 ± 0.022 (0-0.1, release sample: 0.057), b_gg 0.76 against 0.70 from the mocks (was 0.28 against 0.44), and its unweighted stellar-density trend S_1D fell from 177 to 7.9. Other shells' w rose by 1-2σ. The weights (ISD, as in DES Y3/Y6; src/lss/isd.py, citing arXiv:2105.13540 §5.3.1 and 2402.10697 §V.B): eight survey-property maps at nside 256 are built (src/lss/sysmaps.py): E(B−V) (CSFD), Gaia G < 21 star density, SPHEREx mean channel count, |ecliptic latitude|, zodi, annulus background, n_obs, Euclid VIS depth (VMPZ). Five are regressed (template_set fiducial_plus_euclid_depth): E(B−V), stars, channel count, ecliptic latitude, VIS depth. Per shell, galaxy density is measured in 10 equal-area bins of each map; a linear F(s) (intercept 1) is fitted by GLS and its significance is S_1D = Δχ²/Δχ²_68, with the covariance and the 68th percentile from 400 footprint-matched lognormal mocks (pyccl Limber C_ℓ amplitude-matched to the data, FLASK-style lognormal, Poisson sampling) — mocks, not permutations, because a permutation null has no clustering and over-flags cosmic variance. The most significant map is corrected and the loop repeats until every S_1D < 2; each galaxy gets w = 1/F at its pixel, mean 1, clipped to 0.2-5. On 50 clean mocks the same loop flags at least one map in 26-31 of them, so a flag alone is not evidence of contamination. The near-infrared has to be observed from space

Redrawn for this deck from the CosmoStat journal-club figure (scripts/make_intro_svgs.py imports journal_clubs/spherex/scripts/make_em_spectrum.py and swaps the palette). Facility ranges are real; the depth of the opacity curve is schematic, the windows are in the right places. Euclid is picked out beside SPHEREx for this room. Read left to right, then land on the orange column. - From the ground there are two windows, optical and radio; everything else is absorbed. - Between 1 and 5 µm the atmosphere is chopped into narrow windows (J, H, K, L, M) by water vapour and CO2, and beyond ~2.5 µm the sky and the telescope glow. Ground-based near-IR spectroscopy works, but slowly, and never over the whole sky. - So the near-IR belongs to space: JWST, Euclid, Roman, Spitzer, WISE. All but WISE are pointed instruments with small fields. - SPHEREx is not new because of its band. It is new because it takes a spectrum of every point on the sky: WISE did the sky in 4 broad bands, SPHEREx does it in 102 channels. - Euclid and SPHEREx overlap from 0.75 to 2 µm (VIS, Y, J, H), and SPHEREx carries on to 5 µm.

SPHEREx depth

Euclid depth is uniform (tiling at constant exposure), varying weakly with Galactic latitude. SPHEREx revisits the ecliptic poles constantly, so depth is a function of ecliptic latitude: 252 obs at 30°, 325 at 70°, 1,277 beyond 80°. The footprint stops at 87.8° because the poles are Euclid deep fields. The two are essentially uncorrelated (report Fig. 6), so they act as independent axes of the selection function. Median co-added S/N over all targets in the v2 catalogue 11.0 (16-84%: 4.7-34.8); median 333 observations per target. Median E(B-V) 0.022 (CSFD, over targets). This depth gradient comes back in the homogeneity section: it reaches the redshift quality. Figures: made for this talk from the catalogue (n_obs_bkg and snr columns), both lobes in their own Lambert equal-area projection on one angular scale, 0.5° pixels. The bright spot where the rays converge in the south is the deep sky toward the south ecliptic pole. The dark dots are bright-star holes. Bottom right, the Euclid VIS 5σ point-source depth (src/depth_maps.py, nside 64, from per-object flux errors; report Figure 4) in the same projection. Its whole colour scale is 23.6 to 24.1, half a magnitude, and the map is nearly flat inside it — that is the contrast the slide is making. The moth-eaten look is the nside-64 pixelisation of a footprint with holes, not missing depth.

The 12 templates: six quiescent, six star-forming

fsps_QSF_12_v3 is eazy-py's standard FSPS template set (G. Brammer): "QSF" = quiescent + star-forming. My understanding is that it was built by reducing a large FSPS model grid to 12 components, as the original eazy set was; worth checking the eazy-photoz README before saying so in the room. eazy fits a NON-NEGATIVE linear combination of all 12 at each redshift, so the basis spans a continuous space of SEDs, not 12 fixed galaxies. That is why 12 is enough, and why larger banks added variance rather than accuracy in the sweep. Per template (from the headers eazy reads; sSFR = SFR/mass, M/L_V = mass/L_V, EW(Hα) in Å): 1. quiescent, sSFR 8e-12/yr, A_V 0.06, M/L_V 0.39, EW(Hα) 3: youngest of the quiescent set, post-starburst-like, strong Balmer break. 2. quiescent, sSFR 3e-12, A_V 0.11, M/L_V 0.75. 3. quiescent, sSFR 9e-13, A_V 0.13, M/L_V 1.6. 4. quiescent, sSFR 2e-13, A_V 0.12, M/L_V 3.6: oldest, the classic red-sequence SED. 5. quiescent + some dust, sSFR 6e-13, A_V 0.72, M/L_V 5.9. 6. very dusty old population, sSFR 2e-13, A_V 2.88, M/L_V 40. 7. star-forming, sSFR 4e-9, A_V 0.07, EW(Hα) 414, M/L_V 0.12: young, blue, strongest lines. 8. star-forming, sSFR 2e-9, A_V 0.27, EW(Hα) 283. 9. star-forming, sSFR 3e-9, A_V 0.79, EW(Hα) 370. 10. star-forming, sSFR 3e-9, A_V 1.35, EW(Hα) 338. 11. star-forming, sSFR 1e-9, A_V 1.80, EW(Hα) 209. 12. dusty star-forming, sSFR 1e-9, A_V 2.94, EW(Hα) 243, M/L_V 3.8. Physical parameters (logmass, logsfr, Av) in the catalogue are the coefficient-weighted sums of these header values; that is how the AGN bank broke masses when its templates had no sidecar. The rising tail beyond ~4 µm in the star-forming (and dusty) templates is hot dust / PAH emission; the 3.3 µm PAH feature sits inside the SPHEREx range at z < 0.5 but was not measurable per object (AGN arm: AUC 0.43). Template files: data/fsps_QSF_12_v3/ in this deck folder; figure made by make_figures.py (fig_templates).

The stellar grid: BT-Settl model atmospheres, 400–7,000 K

eazy's fit_phoenix_stars loads bt-settl_t400-7000_g4.5.fits: BT-Settl model atmospheres at log g 4.5 (with 4.0-5.5 available), solar metallicity, 400-7,000 K, and by default adds the Sonora brown-dwarf models (Marley et al. 2018 Zenodo release, published as Marley et al. 2021) and a carbon star. eazy fits each as a single template, scaled, with no redshift. The curves here are BT-Settl (AGSS2009 solar abundances) from the SVO Theory Service at log g 4.5, 2,500-7,000 K, binned to R = 300. Same model family as eazy's grid; eazy's exact file is not re-downloadable (its S3 URL returns 403), so small differences in abundance version are possible. Why the flag works where it does: below ~4,000 K the water bands at 1.4, 1.9 and 2.7 µm carve peaks no galaxy template has; at 6,000-7,000 K the spectrum is a smooth Rayleigh-Jeans fall, which a low-z galaxy can mimic, so hot stars are the hard case (Gaia calibration: 0.91 completeness at 18 ≤ I_E < 19).
